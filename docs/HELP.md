# warptempo_gui

warptempo_gui time-warps a recording toward the tempos you choose. It was built for taking commercial orchestral recordings to historically informed tempos, phrase by phrase, by ear.

The engine is a PGHI phase vocoder (Prusa and Holighaus's phase-gradient heap integration, "Phase Vocoder Done Right") run at the authors' own parameters, N = 4096 with 75 % overlap, at the source's own sample rate, and extended in two ways: the analysis hop varies frame by frame with the warp map, and phase resets are placed by hand. Those two and its other departures from the paper (the output timing contract, the pinned randomness of quiet bins) are deliberate and recorded in the engine's own comments. A render is a finished 24-bit WAV through an unconditional limiter, and it is deterministic: the same project on the same machine and build renders the same bytes every time.

There are two products over one interface: the desktop GUI (Wayland and JACK) and the same GUI as an Android APK; a headless CLI renders the same bytes as the GUI. Installing, building, the first run, the tablet and all upkeep are in [`INSTALL.md`](INSTALL.md).

The interface documents itself. With the tooltip lamp lit (Toggle Tooltips, the bare `\`, in the icon row), resting the pointer over any button, or hovering the S Pen over it, names its act and its key. This guide therefore names acts, never keys, and holds only what the tooltips cannot show: what the markers mean and how a movement is worked.

## How the program says no

When a key or a click refuses, the program says why in a card at the top right of the window. Silence means the press was unbound, or a command that acts in one place was already at its state there. So this guide lists no refusals: the program tells you at the moment it matters. A field that refuses what was entered also selects its whole text, so the next keystroke replaces it.

A card leaves on its own after a few seconds, waiting while the pointer rests on it or a press holds it; a few failures, such as a checkpoint that did not reach GitHub, stay until dismissed. A click or tap anywhere on a card dismisses it, and a Shift-click or a long press on any card dismisses them all, as Esc does on the keyboard.

## The concepts

### Warp markers

A warp marker sets the playback rate from its position until the next marker's. It carries a tempo, a stretch ratio in which 1.00 is the recording as it was made. The tempo may be written as a main speed plus a few signed adjustments that add up to it, so a section's headline speed stays visible beside the corrections made to it, and a marker may carry a fine scale on top, for matching a neighbouring section's effective tempo. A disabled marker contributes nothing, as if it were not there. Positions are authored in source view, the unprocessed recording, where a marker is pinned against an acoustic event: a little ahead of it, in the tail of what precedes, a far steadier cue than the silence before an attack. Tempos are judged in target view, the recording as it will render.

### Inheritance

A marker with no tempo of its own, a pass, takes the tempo of the nearest earlier marker that owns one; disabled markers and label references are passed over, and a pass with no owner before it plays as recorded. The resolution is live: disable or delete the owner and every pass after it re-resolves to the next owner back.

### Label definitions and references

This is the program's reason to exist. A marker can define a named label, and a later marker can reference it. The reference renders its own segment at whatever tempo gives that segment the definition's duration, so editing the definition moves every reference with it; disabling the definition disables its references too. That is what ties a recapitulation to its exposition, or a repeat to its first time, through every later edit. A reference is confirmed against its definition by ear with the A/B audition: with one tab parked at the definition and the other at the reference, it plays the same short span from each tab's playhead, back to back, and any press on the window ends it, for it is meant to be heard hands-off.

### Phase resets

Phase resets are a separate collection of markers, authored in either audio view: in target view, where they are protected by ear, and in source view, where they are transcribed against the recording's own attacks. The vocoder's frame-to-frame phase coherence keeps sustained sound clean but smears an attack; at a phase reset it re-seats its phase instead, so the attack (an entry, a pizzicato, a timpani stroke) arrives clean. A reset belongs a lead-in ahead of its transient, and the drop does that arithmetic for you in either view, taking the lead-in once; a drag or a nudge moves a reset exactly where it is put, and the lead-in's dotted outline is drawn in target view alone. Each one trades a little local coherence for the clean attack, so they are placed one by one, and nudged only when a dip, pop or crackle is heard.

### Two tabs, four views

The two tabs, A and B, are two viewpoints (viewport, zoom, playhead and trim) over one shared set of markers, so one can sit at a definition while the other sits at its reference. The active tab's name, "Tab A" or "Tab B", stands in the bottom row right of the clock; Switch Tab in the bottom row and Ctrl+Tab switch to the other tab, and a Shift-press or a long press on Switch Tab walks both tabs, stepping each to its next marker and centering it, and comes back to the tab it started from (Ctrl+Shift+Tab). The view is chosen by two pairs of buttons at the right end of the icon row, each pair a radio with one member down: Source and Target (the bare keys 1 and 2) choose the audio, Warp and Phase Reset (3 and 4) the markers, each key changing its own half and leaving the other alone; pressing the member already down pops it out and selects its partner, the keys alike. Their four combinations are the four views: source + warp, where warp markers are placed; source + phase reset, where resets are transcribed against the recording; target + warp, where tempos are judged; and target + phase reset, where the transients are protected.

The bottom row carries the clock, the active tab's name and the progress line at the left (a press on the clock plays and stops, as PLAY does), and four runs of keys at the right: START and END, which move the playhead to the trim's begin and end (a Shift-press or a long press goes to the piece's own start or end instead, past the trim); the marker verbs DROP, DELETE, DISABLE, INHERIT, JUMP, EDIT, ADD SEL and DRAG; DOWN, UP, PREV and NEXT; and TAB, CENTER, LEFT, RIGHT and PLAY. The icon row opens with OPEN, SAVE, UNDO, REDO, COPY and RENDER, where COPY copies the phase resets of the selected run of warp markers (a press with Ctrl pastes them, Ctrl+Shift pastes their enabled / disabled state). In the icon row, IN and OUT (Zoom In and Zoom Out), left of FIT, zoom the waveform in or out about its middle, each press twice as close or twice as far and a Shift-press or a long press four times, stopping at the deepest zoom and at the whole piece; FIT (Zoom to Fit) shows the whole piece and, pressed again, goes back to the view it left — a zoom in between, by IN or by any other zoom, ends that return, and the next FIT shows the whole piece again; a Shift-press or a long press frames the trim, as a double-click on the trim bar does, and a Ctrl+Shift-press resets the trim to the whole piece. On the glass, with the S Pen's button held, a tap on the trim bar sets the trim's begin and a long press sets its end. A key's legend names what its next press does, so a few change with the state: RENDER reads CANCEL while a single render runs (a batch's progress box has its own Cancel, and RENDER keeps its word behind it), SAVE reads COMMIT in the history view (PULL there when GitHub is ahead, and CHECK when the press would ask GitHub again: while offline, and on the laptop while up to date), FIT reads RECALL when the press would go back to the view it left, PLAY reads STOP while playing, INHERIT reads TIE under the lit GRID lamp, and the player's PLAY reads PAUSE or OPEN (over a folder); a toggle keeps its one legend, its key down and its light on while it is on.

TRIM in the icon row (`t`), after FOLLOW, opens the piece's list of named trim presets in the box over the waveform, each line a preset's name, in the order of the music — by where each begins, the shorter first of two that begin together; the list opens on the preset that matches the current tab's trim, or on the first. A tap on a line (or Enter) applies that preset to the current tab's trim and frames it, as a double-click on the trim bar does, and the list stays, its line chosen; the new framing shows when the list closes. DOWN and UP at the right below the list (Down and Up too) move the choice without applying anything, which is the way to a preset you mean to save into or delete. SAVE gives the chosen preset the current trim, its name kept, and DELETE removes it, each after asking, with Cancel the answer ready; SAVE is dimmed on a preset that already holds the current trim, and with no presets yet it does what SAVE AS does. SAVE AS asks for a name and saves the current trim under it as a new preset, the new line chosen when the list comes back (a name must be new to the list, not empty, and must not begin or end with a space; Cancel comes back to the list with nothing saved). TRIM again, CLOSE or Esc close the list. The presets live beside the piece, in its folder's `trim/presets.txt`, and are kept at once on this device, never saved with the piece, committed or carried to the other device, like the score's page range; on a read-only tab and while grid iterations is on the list works as ever, the trim being the tab's viewpoint rather than the piece.

Save is the unsaved-work mark: it lights when the markers or the piece's settings block (the scale, the BPM and the rest) change, and is dimmed again once saved or undone back to the saved state. The tabs' viewpoints, their trims and their read-only locks never light it; they are written with the next save of such a change, and a trim or lock changed on its own is not kept.

### History

A piece's checkpoints are git commits in the projects repository. The tablet authors and commits (`Ctrl+Shift+S`, Save and Commit, in the main window or the history view, under the title `Update <piece>` with nothing asked; it pulls instead when GitHub is ahead), GitHub is the hub, and the laptop pulls (the same key there; each device's config names its role, author or mirror). `Ctrl+S` is the plain save everywhere. The history view walks the checkpoints and shows each as a diff of flags, from which a difference can be reverted or a whole checkpoint loaded in place; a moved marker shows both of its positions, the old one's label struck through, joined by a dotted ring, and Tab visits each; the mechanics are in [`INSTALL.md`](INSTALL.md)'s Daily use. The view reopens on the checkpoint it was last viewing, even after a new checkpoint, until another piece is opened. CUMUL (Toggle Cumulative) compares every step against the current state instead of against the next newer one, and keeps that reading until it is pressed again or the program closes. SESSION (Toggle Session History) walks this session's own undo and redo timeline instead of the checkpoints, one step per state Undo and Redo can reach, and the view opens on it by itself where the device has no clone to read the checkpoints from.

### Settings

The toolbar's Settings button (`;`) opens the settings list in the box over the waveform: each of the thirteen settings below as its line, `key=value` with the value it holds now, the device's under DEVICE and the piece's under PIECE (the two headings take no press). While the list stands the button stays lit, and pressing it again (or Esc, or Close) closes it. A tap on a line (or Up and Down to it, then Enter) closes the list and opens that line in the box, ready to edit, the cursor at its end; OK (or Enter) applies it and Cancel (or Esc) leaves everything as it was, and either way the settings list comes back on that line. Five lines list their setting's values instead, in the settings list's place, the value it holds now chosen and in the middle: `waveform` the waveform presets by name (Colors, below), `true_colors` `on` and `off`, `noise` the folders under `noise/`, `noise_level` 0 down to -30 in steps of 1, and `noise_crossfade_s` 1 to 30, each value written as in its line (the level being in decibels and the crossfade in seconds). A tap on a value (or Enter) applies it at once, keeps it for the next launch and returns to the settings list, on that setting's line, now showing the new value; the value already held changes nothing and returns the same; Esc (or Cancel) returns to the settings list with nothing applied; and the Settings button closes the whole list, as over the settings list itself. These five can still be typed in a line, as below. In the open line Tab completes as you go: the beginning of a key becomes the key and `=` when only one key starts that way (`wave` becomes `waveform=`), or goes on as far as the keys starting that way agree (`proj` stops at `projects_`, `projects_repo` and `projects_path` both being keys); on a whole key Tab adds the `=` alone; after a key and `=`, the next Tab adds the value the key holds now, to be edited rather than retyped; after `waveform=` it completes what you have typed of a preset's key, after `true_colors=` of `on` or `off`, after `projects_path=` of a folder's name, and after `noise=` of a folder's name under `noise/`. A Tab that adds nothing moves on to OK. The keys Tab knows: `title`, `notes`, `url` and `cover`, the piece's own, saved with it and undone with Ctrl+Z; `score_path`, the piece's score folder (Score, below), read at once and kept at once on this device, never with the save; `gui_scale`, the window's scale, applied at once; `projects_repo` and `projects_path`, where the projects live (a new path takes effect at the next Open Project or launch, as a card says); `waveform`, the waveform preset (Colors, below); `true_colors`, `on` or `off`; and `noise`, `noise_level` and `noise_crossfade_s`, the sound's folder, its loudness and its crossfade (Noise, below), applied at once — everything after the piece's five belonging to the device, kept in its config for the next launch. A word that is no key, or a value its key refuses, is refused, its reason on a card and the whole line selected. On a read-only tab, and while grid iterations is on, the list and its lines still open: the device's keys apply as ever, and so does `score_path`, the score folder being kept on this device and not with the piece; the piece's other four, `title`, `notes`, `url` and `cover`, are refused, a card saying why, until the lock is lifted.

### Score

A movement's sheet music can stand beside it: the SCORE key in the icon row (`m`) opens the score viewer, which shows the score one page at a time in the box over the waveform, each page centered and scaled to fit the box, and closes it again (so do CLOSE and Esc). The pages are a folder made by `tools/score_extract.py` ([`INSTALL.md`](INSTALL.md)) from a sheet-music video, or from a PDF of the printed score: then each page the viewer shows is a stretch of a printed page cut between its systems (the next system's top may show at the bottom, and on an occasional page, a recitative's, the cut may fall through a staff or repeat part of another), every one at the same size so the staves never grow or shrink as you turn, and the PDF's pages without music (the cover, a preface) are left out; a piece whose own folder holds them as `score/` shows them with nothing typed. When one video carries the whole symphony, the other movements name the first one's folder with the setting `score_path`, relative to the project's folder (`../550 - 1/score`); an empty `score_path=` turns the score off. With no score, or a score folder the program cannot read, the SCORE key is dimmed and the piece opens as ever. The viewer numbers the pages its own way, 1 to the last, never by the numbers printed on them. The viewer is a player of its own: it plays the score folder's `audio.wav`, the video's soundtrack, and the page on screen is always the page that sound belongs to, turning by itself as it plays. Below the page, PLAY starts it and PAUSE stops it (Space too), BACK and FWD either side of it skip three seconds back and forward, ten with a long press, the slider is a media player's, moving with the sound and seeking where you let go of it, the page under its handle shown as you drag it (a playing sound pauses while the slider is held and plays on from where it is let go), and the two boxes beside it read the time into the page range and the range's length; Home and End go to the range's first and last moment. When it reaches the range's end it stops dead and rests there, the last page on screen (a slider let go at the end does the same); PLAY there plays the range again from its start. Nothing in the viewer follows the piece: the piece's own playback stops when the viewer opens, its playhead stays where it was, and keeping the two together is yours to do by hand. A score folder with no playable `audio.wav` (a PDF's never has one) is turned by hand instead: PREV and NEXT turn one page (Left and Right too, and Home and End go to the first and last), the slider shows the page under its handle as you drag it, and the box beside it reads which page of how many, counted within the page range. The page range is how a movement is cut out of the whole: START makes the page on screen the range's first, END its last, and RESET, after asking (a playing soundtrack pauses for the question), opens the range to every page again; once set, the player and its slider stay inside the range and nothing outside it plays. Like the trim they have no undo, and they are kept at once; on a read-only tab or while grid iterations is on they are dimmed, while turning pages and playing stay free. Each tab remembers where it was — the moment in the soundtrack, or the page when there is none — written as you go, and the viewer opens there.

### Noise

A bed of sound — rain, or whatever the chosen folder holds — can play under the work, to mask the room: the NOISE key in the icon row (`w`), last of its run after SCORE, lights it and, pressed again, puts it out. Lit, the sound plays continuously, under everything the program plays — the piece, the A/B audition, the render player, the score's soundtrack — and through the silence between them, at one level that nothing playing ever lowers or pauses; only the key, a change of sound device (below), a change of the setting `noise`, or closing the program stops it. It is a set of recordings played one after another in random order, never the same one twice in a row, each fading into the next over a few seconds, and every press starts afresh with one of them from its beginning; their own differences in loudness are kept, as nature's are. A folder of one recording loops it onto itself through the same fade. It fades in over a fifth of a second at the lit press, once its recordings are loaded (the first press reads them, so its light comes on before the sound), and out almost at once at the dark press; a press during either fade turns it back from where it is. If the sound device changes (headphones connected or taken off), the sound stops and its key goes dark, as the piece stops; press the key again to start it. It is off at every launch. The recordings are a folder `noise/<name>/` beside the device config ([`INSTALL.md`](INSTALL.md)), one folder per sound, and the setting `noise` names the one that plays (its line on the settings list lists the folders there, and Tab completes a folder's name in a typed line); changing `noise` puts the sound out, drops its recordings from memory and reads the new folder at the next press. The named folder's recordings are kept in memory from the first press until the program closes or the folder changes; nothing limits their size, and about 35 minutes, about 400 MB at 48 kHz, is a sensible ceiling. The setting `noise_level` is its loudness in decibels, from -30 to 0 in whole decibels, applied to the recordings as they are: 0 plays them as recorded and -9, the starting value, plays a moment that reached full scale in its file at about a third of full scale; `noise_crossfade_s` is the length of the fade from one recording to the next, in whole seconds from 1 to 30 (4 to begin with; a sound of other recordings may want another). Each of the three lists its values on the settings list (Settings, above), a tap applying one; all three apply at once and are kept on the device. With no folder of that name, or one the program cannot read, the NOISE key is dimmed. The sound and the music are added together, and a sum louder than full scale is clipped in the program rather than at the speaker.

## A working method

How a movement actually gets authored, in the order that works:

1. Find the introduction measures that define the tempo. Set warp markers there, set the corresponding phase resets, then run BPM iterations and pick the tempo that sits right.
2. Work down the piece in chronological order, moving between the views (1 / 2 for source or target, 3 / 4 for warp or phase reset): source + warp to place markers, target + warp for tempo adjustments by ear, and target + phase reset to protect the transients (source + phase reset when a reset is better placed against the recording itself).
3. Once some headway is made, copy label definitions into references (the exposition into the repeat and the recapitulation), and paste the definition's phase resets across with the propagate commands, all from the warp view: the bottom row's COPY key copies the phase resets of a run of selected warp markers, a press with Ctrl pastes them onto one selected warp marker, and a press with Ctrl+Shift pastes their enabled / disabled state alone onto the matching resets there. On the glass the S Pen's button is Ctrl: a tap on COPY copies, a tap with the pen's button held pastes, and a long press with the pen's button held pastes the state.
4. Phase resets are set with the phase-reset drop and nudged only when the phase alignment causes an audible dip, pop or crackle.
5. The A/B audition is crucial for confirming that label references match their definitions: it is the crux of the tempo-locking mechanism, which lets precise timing be replicated in the sonata-form fashion of repeated rhythmic motifs and phrases, at both large and small scale.
6. Grid and BPM iterations, trimmed target previews and the history view carry the rest: render a spread (a box counts the cells as they render, its Cancel stops the spread, and the render player opens on the spread when the last cell is done), listen in the render player, load the winner in place, checkpoint.

On timing: the scanner's smooth movement lets a warp marker land essentially at the onset of an attack, so place against what you see and hear together.

## The reference project

`projects/550 - 1/` in the [projects repository](https://github.com/warptempo/warptempo_projects), the first movement of Symphony No. 40, is the worked example and exercises everything: owning and inheriting markers, disabled markers, label definitions and references, a tempo written as a main speed plus an adjustment term, tempos fine-tuned with a per-marker scale, phase resets placed under masking, and a complete settings block. See it wherever a number or a spelling matters. ## Where the rest lives

- [`INSTALL.md`](INSTALL.md): installing, building, the first run, the tablet, daily use, trouble, and migrating an older project folder.
- The tooltips: every button and its key, with the tooltip lamp lit.
- There is no menu bar: Open Project is the toolbar's first button (Ctrl+O; its Shift-press or long press is Revert, Ctrl+Alt+O). It opens the project list, and while the list stands the key stays lit and pressing it again (or Ctrl+O, or Esc) closes the list, while its Shift-press or long press (or Ctrl+Alt+O) still reverts. A question about unsaved work — choosing another project, or reverting — stands over the list: Cancel (or Esc) returns to the list as it was, and the answer that goes ahead reopens the program on the project. Quit is Ctrl+Q, Back on the tablet or the close button of the laptop's title bar (the window manager's), and Settings is the toolbar's Settings button (`;`).
- The dialogs: a button's underlined letter is its access key — the letter alone answers a question, Alt with the letter while a text field has the focus; Enter is the focused button and Esc is Cancel.
- A label being edited in the marker lane (a marker's, or a grid iteration's bound): Enter keeps it and Esc leaves it as it was, and so does a press or tap on the waveform or the lanes above it, which does nothing else; everything else in the window waits until the label is closed, the on-screen keyboard's keys aside.
- The source comments and `docs/engineering/closed_questions.md`: the engineering record of the rulings behind the behaviour, not user reading.
- [`README.md`](../README.md): credits and licence.
